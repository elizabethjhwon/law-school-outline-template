\section{Linguistic (Semantic) Canons}

\subsection{How to Use Linguistic Canons}

Linguistic or semantic canons are heuristics for decoding legal language. The basic premise is that statutory enactment is a form of communication: legislators and readers rely on shared conventions about word meaning, grammar, syntax, and context. These canons are therefore supposed to capture ordinary practices of understanding language, not operate as rigid legal commands.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{What they do:} Linguistic canons help determine meaning from grammar, word choice, lists, neighboring provisions, and the relationship among parts of a statute.
    \item \textbf{What they do not do:} They do not automatically override clear text, statutory context, or the statute's evident function.
    \item \textbf{Main criticism:} Courts can select among competing canons to justify a preferred result. The same word or list may support different inferences depending on how the court characterizes the provision.
    \item \textbf{No meta-rule:} There is no formal rule for choosing among otherwise compatible canons. Judges must use context and common sense to select the reading that best fits the statutory communication.
    \item \textbf{Legislative-drafting value:} Making ordinary linguistic conventions explicit may help Congress anticipate how readers will understand statutory language.
    \item \textbf{Exam method:} State the text; identify the canon; explain the inference; give the strongest response; then ask which reading better fits the statute as a whole.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Ordinary Meaning and Special or Technical Meaning}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ordinary meaning:} Words are generally read according to their ordinary public meaning at the time of enactment, in the context in which they appear.
    \item A dictionary may be evidence of ordinary meaning, but it does not answer the question by itself. The relevant meaning may depend on grammar, subject matter, surrounding words, and the legal or commercial setting.
    \item \textbf{Special or technical meaning:} A term may carry a technical meaning in a particular industry, legal field, or statutory scheme. The party invoking a technical meaning must connect it to the statute's context rather than simply assert that experts use the term differently.
    \item \textbf{Arguments for ordinary meaning:} It promotes notice, predictability, and judicial restraint; it keeps interpretation tied to enacted language rather than unenacted purpose.
    \item \textbf{Arguments against a purely ordinary-meaning approach:} Ordinary language is often context-dependent; dictionary definitions may list several meanings; and a technical or historical meaning may better reflect how Congress used the term.
    \item \textbf{Meaning at enactment:} Courts often ask what the relevant audience would have understood the words to mean when enacted. But a term may be elastic if Congress used it as a term of art that incorporates a changing legal or technical standard.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Case illustrations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Nix v. Hedden}: ``Tomato'' was treated according to its ordinary commercial meaning rather than its botanical classification. The takeaway is that ordinary meaning is a use-based and contextual inquiry.
    \item \textit{Corning Glass Works v. Brennan}: The meaning of ``outside salesman'' depended on the statute's wage-and-hour structure and the work Congress sought to regulate, not merely on the broadest dictionary definition.
    \item \textit{McBoyle v. United States}: ``Vehicle'' in a criminal statute was read in light of the words surrounding it and did not extend to airplanes. The reasoning combines ordinary meaning, contextual association, and the rule of lenity.
    \item \textit{Allied-Bruce Terminix v. Dobson} and the discussion preceding \textit{Circuit City}: ``Involving commerce'' was treated as an elastic term of art that tracks the scope of Congress's Commerce Clause authority. The competing argument is that the statute should instead be read according to what Congress could have understood the term to cover at enactment.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Whole-Text and Contextual Canons}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Whole-text principle:} Read a provision in relation to the statute's definitions, neighboring provisions, exceptions, remedies, and overall structure.
    \item \textbf{Harmonization:} Prefer an interpretation that allows provisions to operate together rather than making one provision undermine another.
    \item \textbf{Consistent usage:} The same term ordinarily has the same meaning throughout a statute, while materially different terms may signal materially different meanings.
    \item \textbf{Arguments for:} A statute is an integrated enactment, not a collection of isolated sentences; context makes interpretation more faithful to the enacted design.
    \item \textbf{Arguments against overuse:} Courts may invoke ``context'' to move away from text, import policy judgments, or treat similar words as different without a sufficiently concrete reason.
    \item The textbook's general point is that these canons are often ordinary contextual inferences under Latin names. The label matters less than explaining the communication practice that makes the inference plausible.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Case illustrations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Services}: The majority read the amount-in-controversy requirement together with the supplemental-jurisdiction statute and allowed jurisdiction over additional plaintiffs when at least one plaintiff satisfied the jurisdictional minimum. The dissent argued that the Court's structural reading weakened the ordinary requirement that each claim independently satisfy the jurisdictional rules.
    \item \textit{Gustafson v. Alloyd Co.}: ``Prospectus'' was read in the context of the Securities Act's disclosure and registration scheme rather than at its broadest possible level. The Court used neighboring provisions and statutory structure to narrow the term.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Surplusage and Meaningful Variation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rule against surplusage:} Courts should, if possible, give effect to every word and avoid an interpretation that makes language meaningless or redundant.
    \item \textbf{Meaningful variation:} When Congress uses different words in nearby provisions, the difference may indicate a difference in meaning.
    \item \textbf{For the canon:} It respects the premise that Congress writes purposefully and prevents courts from erasing enacted language.
    \item \textbf{Against the canon:} Statutes contain repetition, belt-and-suspenders drafting, and imperfect compromises. Avoiding every redundancy can produce an unnatural reading or defeat the statute's evident purpose.
    \item The canon is strongest when the competing interpretation makes a word do no work at all; it is weaker when the word still performs an overlapping or emphasizing function.
    \item The textbook questions whether ordinary speakers really avoid redundancy and notes that statutory drafting may deliberately repeat or overlap terms. Surplusage is therefore a presumption, not proof that Congress could not have intended the broader reading.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Noscitur a Sociis}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rule:} A word is known by the company it keeps. A potentially broad term may be given a narrower meaning when its neighboring terms share a recognizable subject or function.
    \item \textbf{Use:} Identify the common feature of the associated words and ask whether the disputed word should be understood as part of that same group.
    \item \textbf{For the canon:} It prevents a general word from swallowing the more specific words around it and uses the enacted list as evidence of meaning.
    \item \textbf{Against the canon:} The associated words may not share a sufficiently clear category; narrowing one word may improperly add a limitation Congress did not write.
    \item The canon is especially useful when a broad term appears beside terms that reveal a common subject. It is much less useful when the surrounding words do not establish a coherent class.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Ejusdem Generis}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rule:} When specific words are followed by general words, the general words are ordinarily limited to things of the same general kind as the specific words.
    \item \textbf{Example:} ``Cars, trucks, wagons, or other vehicles'' ordinarily points toward other land transportation, not every means of conveyance.
    \item The canon depends on a genuine list of specific examples followed by a residual term. It is less persuasive when the statute signals breadth through terms such as ``any,'' provides a broad definition, or uses the general term independently elsewhere.
    \item \textbf{For the canon:} It prevents a broad residual phrase from making the specific examples unnecessary and reflects how people ordinarily use illustrative lists.
    \item \textbf{Against the canon:} It can defeat a deliberately broad phrase and force a category onto examples that Congress may have listed for emphasis rather than limitation.
    \item \textbf{Main difficulty:} The interpreter must extract the characteristic shared by the listed examples. The result may be narrow or broad depending on how that characteristic is described.
    \item Specific examples may also have been included out of an abundance of caution rather than to define a category. If so, the general term may remain broad despite the list.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Case illustrations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{People v. Smith}: ``Dagger, dirk, stiletto, or other dangerous weapon'' was read through ejusdem generis. The specific weapons informed the kind of danger covered by ``other,'' rather than leaving the residual phrase unlimited.
    \item \textit{Circuit City Stores v. Adams}: ``Any other class of workers engaged in foreign or interstate commerce'' was limited to workers in the transportation industry because it followed specific transportation workers. The dissent argued that ``any other class'' was deliberately broad and that the majority improperly added a transportation limitation.
    \item \textit{McBoyle}: The surrounding list of ground transportation terms supported reading ``vehicle'' in a narrower, transportation-specific way; the criminal context supplied an additional reason not to extend the statute by analogy.
    \item The textbook also connects \textit{Circuit City} to surplusage: under the majority's reading, the specific references to seamen and railroad employees still do work by defining the scope of the broader transportation category. The dissent can respond that the specific terms become redundant if the general category already covers all workers Congress could regulate.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Expressio Unius Est Exclusio Alterius}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rule:} Express mention of one item may imply the exclusion of other items.
    \item \textbf{Strongest use:} The statute creates a closed list, enumerates exceptions to a general rule, or carefully distinguishes among categories.
    \item \textbf{Weakest use:} The list is illustrative, the statute uses ``including'' or ``such as,'' the omitted item is not comparable to the listed items, or the omission may simply reflect drafting economy.
    \item \textbf{For the canon:} It treats omissions as potentially deliberate and prevents courts from adding exceptions or categories that Congress chose not to include.
    \item \textbf{Against the canon:} Silence is often ambiguous; Congress may not have considered the omitted item, and an exclusion inference can frustrate a statute's remedial purpose.
    \item The canon is strongest when the statutory list appears carefully designed or when the listed item is an exception to a general rule. It is weakest when the statute merely gives examples or when the omission could reflect drafting economy.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Case illustration: \textit{Silvers v. Sony Pictures Entertainment}}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The Copyright Act allowed an action by the ``legal or beneficial owner of an exclusive right.'' Nancy Silvers's assignee held an accrued infringement claim but not an ownership interest in the copyright or an exclusive right.
    \item \textbf{Majority/takeaway:} The statute's deliberate reference to legal and beneficial owners indicated who may sue; Congress did not include an assignee who received only the bare right to sue. The court also read the phrase in light of copyright's division between ownership of rights and enforcement of claims.
    \item \textbf{Counterargument:} The statute does not expressly say that assignees are excluded, and allowing assignment would facilitate efficient enforcement. The majority's inference from omission can be criticized as converting a contextual presumption into a categorical bar.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Modifiers, the Last Antecedent, and Series Qualifiers}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Last-antecedent rule:} A limiting phrase ordinarily modifies only the immediately preceding phrase or noun, especially when punctuation and grammar support that reading.
    \item \textbf{Series-qualifier principle:} When a modifier appears at the end of a parallel series, it may apply to every item in the series when the syntax and shared feature of the series support that reading.
    \item These canons can point in opposite directions. The court should examine punctuation, parallel grammar, the common feature connecting the items, and whether one reading makes the provision unusually arbitrary or irrational.
    \item \textbf{For narrow application:} It follows ordinary grammatical proximity and avoids extending a modifier beyond the words it naturally follows.
    \item \textbf{For broad application:} It treats the series as a unified grammatical unit and avoids making the final modifier apply to only the last item for no apparent reason.
    \item These rules are not interchangeable. The last-antecedent rule focuses on proximity; the series-qualifier principle focuses on parallel structure and the shared feature of the entire series.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Case illustrations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Lockhart v. United States}: The majority applied the last-antecedent rule to read ``involving a minor'' as modifying only the immediately preceding offense. The dissent invoked the series-qualifier principle and argued that the phrase naturally described all offenses in the parallel series.
    \item \textit{Facebook v. Duguid}: The Court applied the series-qualifier principle so that ``using a random or sequential number generator'' modified both ``store'' and ``produce.'' The takeaway is that a trailing modifier can reach across a series when the shared grammatical structure supports it.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Linguistic Canons in Conflict}

\begin{itemize}
    \item A single provision may trigger several canons: ejusdem generis may narrow a general term, while expressio unius may suggest that an unlisted category is excluded, and the whole-text principle may point toward a broader reading.
    \item Courts should not mechanically count canons. The stronger interpretation is usually the one that best fits the text, grammar, statutory structure, and apparent function together.
    \item A canon that produces an implausible or unusually narrow result may be rebutted by clear context, a statutory definition, a broad operative term, or a contrary provision elsewhere in the statute.
    \item In criminal cases, linguistic uncertainty may be reinforced by the rule of lenity. Lenity is a substantive value-based canon, not merely a linguistic rule, because it resolves genuine ambiguity in favor of the defendant.
\end{itemize}

\section{Substantive Canons}
\definition{Substantive Canons}
{These canons ask interpreters to put a thumb on the scale in favor of some value or policy that courts have identified as worthy of protection.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item In order for these canons to do any work, it must be the case that without the use of the canon, the court would have reached a different conclusion.
    \item Substantive canons may also be understood as techniques the courts employ in order to advance particular values that the \textit{courts} have decided are important (rather than Congress).
    \item Unlike linguistic canons, substantive canons do not primarily describe how words work. They change the interpretive baseline because the court is protecting a constitutional value, federalism, individual liberty, or another judicially recognized interest.
    \item \textbf{Legitimacy question:} Courts must explain why the value is sufficiently grounded in the Constitution, a longstanding legal principle, or an established doctrinal presumption to justify departing from the most natural reading.
\end{itemize}
\question{Legitimacy}{Under what conditions is it legitimate for courts to develop and apply substantive canons?}

\subsection{Avoiding Constitutional Questions}

\subcanon{Avoidance Canon}
{
\begin{itemize}
    \item Courts should construe statutes to avoid serious constitutional problems when a constitutionally safer construction is fairly possible.
    \item \textbf{Classical avoidance:} Choose the interpretation that avoids an actual constitutional violation. This approach assumes the court will decide whether the rejected construction is unconstitutional.
    \item \textbf{Modern avoidance:} A serious constitutional doubt may be enough to support a different, fairly possible interpretation, allowing the court to avoid deciding the constitutional question.
    \item The canon normally requires more than a merely conceivable constitutional problem: there must be a substantial likelihood that the otherwise-preferred reading would be unconstitutional. The precise threshold is contested.
    \item The modern canon is more powerful but also more controversial because courts can use constitutional concerns to narrow a statute before determining that the statute would actually be unconstitutional.
    \item \textbf{Legitimating rationale:} It promotes judicial restraint, respects constitutional limits, and avoids unnecessarily invalidating an act of Congress.
    \item \textbf{Criticism:} It may distort statutory meaning, invite judges to manufacture ambiguity, and shift policy choices from Congress to courts.
\end{itemize}
}

\textbf{Additional illustrations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Zadvydas v. Davis}: The Court read a detention statute to authorize detention only for a reasonably necessary period rather than indefinite detention, because the broader reading would raise a serious Due Process problem.
    \item \textit{Clark v. Martinez}: The Court extended the narrowing construction to inadmissible noncitizens, illustrating both the power and the controversy of applying an avoidance construction beyond the precise facts that generated the constitutional concern.
    \item \textbf{Limit:} Avoidance is supposed to select among plausible readings, not license the Court to rewrite a statute or refuse to apply a clear command merely because the result is constitutionally difficult.
\end{itemize}

\casebox{\textit{NLRB v. Catholic Bishop of Chicago}}
{Supreme Court, 1979}
{
\textbf{Takeaway}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The Court used constitutional avoidance to hold that the NLRA did not cover teachers in church-operated schools absent a clear affirmative indication from Congress, avoiding the First Amendment question.
    \item The case illustrates the modern avoidance canon: a serious constitutional concern can affect the statutory choice even when the Court does not hold that the broader reading is unconstitutional.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Arguments about the statute's coverage}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Majority:} The NLRA's broad definition of ``employer'' did not expressly address church-operated schools. Because applying the Act could entangle the government with religious decisions, the Court required a clear congressional indication before finding coverage.
    \item \textbf{Dissent:} The text covered employers generally and listed specific exclusions; church-operated schools were not excluded. The majority effectively added an exemption that Congress had not enacted.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Arguments about avoidance}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Majority:} Regulating teacher employment could require the NLRB to evaluate religious reasons for hiring, firing, or bargaining. Avoiding that serious constitutional problem was preferable to forcing the constitutional issue.
    \item \textbf{Dissent:} Avoidance permits a fairly possible construction, not a judicially created exception. The majority's clear-affirmative-expression requirement went beyond ordinary avoidance and allowed constitutional doubt to displace ordinary text.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Legislative history}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Majority:} Congress had not clearly considered church-operated schools, so legislative history did not supply the affirmative expression needed to support coverage.
    \item \textbf{Dissent:} Congress considered and rejected broader exemptions for nonprofit, religious, and educational organizations, suggesting that Congress meant the general coverage to remain in place unless an exemption was enacted.
\end{itemize}
}

\subsection{State Sovereignty}

\subcanon{Federalism Clear-Statement Canon}
{
\begin{itemize}
    \item When a federal statute would intrude on areas central to state sovereignty, courts may require Congress to speak clearly before reading the statute to produce that result.
    \item This canon is distinct from constitutional avoidance. Avoidance asks whether a construction would likely be unconstitutional; the federalism canon may apply whenever a reading would significantly intrude on a core state function, even without a substantial probability of constitutional invalidity.
    \item The canon protects state authority by resolving genuine ambiguity against an interpretation that displaces state control over core governmental functions.
    \item \textbf{For the canon:} It prevents accidental federal displacement and gives Congress political accountability for decisions that alter the federal-state balance.
    \item \textbf{Against the canon:} It can create a special presumption against federal law even where Congress used broad language and can allow courts to override an enacted federal policy based on their own view of federalism.
    \item \textbf{Political-safeguards response:} One argument against judicially enforced federalism canons is that the States are represented in the national political process, so structural safeguards---not judicially created limits---should protect state interests. The response is that political safeguards may be inadequate when national political forces favor federal regulation.
\end{itemize}
}

\casebox{\textit{Gregory v. Ashcroft}}
{Supreme Court, 1991}
{
\textbf{Takeaway}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The Court held that Missouri's appointed judges fell within the ADEA's exclusion for an ``appointee on the policymaking level.''
    \item The Court also relied on a federalism clear-statement principle: defining the qualifications of a State's most important officials lies at the heart of representative government, so Congress must make its intent to regulate that function unmistakably clear.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Text and structure}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Majority/Ashcroft:} Judges exercise substantial policymaking authority through rulemaking, supervision, and the development of law. ``Policymaking level'' refers to the responsibility of the office, not merely to officials who advise the Governor.
    \item \textbf{Dissent/Gregory:} Judges primarily apply law and are independent from the Governor. Under \textit{noscitur a sociis}, the surrounding exclusions for personal staff and advisers suggested a narrower category of officials closely tied to an elected executive.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Federalism and constitutional avoidance}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Majority/Ashcroft:} The ADEA clearly covered States in general but did not clearly regulate the qualifications of state judges. Defining the qualifications of a State's most important officials is a core state function, so the Court required unmistakable congressional intent before applying the ADEA to judges.
    \item \textbf{Dissent/Gregory:} Congress expressly included States within the ADEA's definition of employer. The remaining issue was the meaning of one statutory exception, not whether Congress had clearly chosen to regulate state government. The majority therefore created an extra clear-statement rule and used federalism to narrow an otherwise applicable federal statute.
    \item \textbf{Important distinction:} The majority's principal move was the federalism clear-statement canon, not a holding that applying the ADEA to judges would necessarily be unconstitutional. The canon operates as a substantive protection of state autonomy even before invalidation is required.
\end{itemize}
}

\subsection{Preemption of State Law}

\subcanon{Presumption Against Preemption}
{
\begin{itemize}
    \item Courts generally presume that Congress does not displace state law, especially state police-power regulation in areas traditionally occupied by the States, unless Congress's purpose to preempt is clear.
    \item The presumption is a substantive federalism canon: it affects the baseline meaning of an ambiguous federal statute in order to protect state authority.
    \item \textbf{Express preemption:} Congress expressly identifies the state-law requirements or categories of state law that federal law displaces. The central questions are the meaning of the preemption clause and whether the challenged state law falls within its scope.
    \item \textbf{Conflict preemption:} State and federal law cannot both be obeyed, or state law stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment of federal objectives.
    \item \textbf{Field preemption:} The federal scheme is so pervasive, or the federal interest so dominant, that Congress implicitly occupied the field and left no room for state regulation.
    \item The interpretive question in each form is what Congress enacted and how broadly the federal law reaches; implied preemption cannot be inferred merely from the existence of federal regulation.
    \item \textbf{For the presumption:} It respects the federal balance and prevents broad federal language from silently eliminating state regulation.
    \item \textbf{Against the presumption:} It may frustrate a federal scheme that Congress intended to be uniform and may make state-law survival depend on a judicially chosen federalism baseline rather than statutory text.
    \item \textbf{Relationship to Gregory:} Preemption often displaces state law rather than directly regulating state governments, but both contexts raise the same basic question: how much clarity should courts require before reading federal law to displace state authority?
\end{itemize}
}

\textbf{Case illustrations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator}: In a field traditionally regulated by the States, the Court began with a presumption against preemption and looked for a clear and manifest congressional purpose. The federal scheme nevertheless displaced state regulation of matters covered by the federal licensing system.
    \item \textit{Cipollone v. Liggett Group}: The Court treated the express preemption clause as controlling and parsed its text claim by claim. Warning-based claims were preempted, but claims based on express warranty, certain fraud theories, and other duties were not automatically displaced. The case shows both textual attention to an express clause and a presumption against broadly expanding it.
    \item \textit{Spector v. Norwegian Cruise Line}: The Court applied the ADA to foreign-flag cruise ships in U.S. waters, while recognizing that some applications affecting a ship's internal affairs could raise distinct concerns. The majority rejected a categorical rule excluding foreign-flag vessels; the dissent would have required a clearer statement before applying the statute in a way that implicated foreign-flag operations.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Scope and Application of Substantive Canons}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Identify the trigger:} Ask what value activates the canon, like religious liberty, state sovereignty, liberty in criminal law, foreign relations, or another established interest.
    \item \textbf{Identify the ambiguity:} A substantive canon should not be used merely because the court dislikes the policy consequences of a clear statute. There must be a genuine alternative reading that the text can fairly support.
    \item \textbf{Distinguish interpretation from invalidation:} Avoidance changes the statutory interpretation to avoid a constitutional question; if no fairly possible construction avoids the problem, the court must confront constitutionality rather than rewrite the statute. Other substantive canons may protect a constitutional value even when no particular statutory application is likely unconstitutional.
    \item \textbf{Scope matters:} The force of a canon may depend on the specific application. A statute may apply generally but not reach a particular state function, claim, foreign activity, or constitutional concern.
    \item \textbf{Clear statement versus presumption:} A clear-statement rule demands a particularly explicit statement from Congress. A presumption supplies a default that can be rebutted by text, structure, purpose, or history. Courts should explain which form they are using.
    \item \textbf{Institutional concern:} The more a canon changes the apparent reach of a statute, the stronger the justification must be. The central question is whether the canon protects a legal value sufficiently important and established to warrant judicially imposed interpretive weight.
    \item \textbf{Canon versus context:} Courts should distinguish a substantive canon from an ordinary contextual inference. Calling a consideration a ``canon'' gives it independent weight; treating it as context makes it one factor in determining ordinary meaning.
    \item \textbf{Doctrinal uncertainty:} The difficult cases ask whether the value is sufficiently grounded in constitutional structure, whether courts are institutionally competent to enforce it, and whether the canon gives Congress fair notice of the clarity required.
\end{itemize}
