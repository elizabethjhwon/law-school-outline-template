\section{Acquisition Through Adverse Possession}
\doctrine{Adverse Possession}
{If the true owner does not timely (statute of limitations) assert their ownership rights, while another person openly treats the land as their own, the law may eventually recognize the possessor as the owner. \\
\textbf{Rule} To constitute adverse possession, there must be actual possession which is uninterrupted, open and notorious, hostile and exclusive, and under a claim of right made in good faith for the statutory period.}

\casebox
{\textit{Scott v. Anderson-Tully Co.} (2015)}
{}
{
    \textbf{Facts}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Scott claimed ownership of a disputed twenty-acre parcel through inheritance
        \item Anderson-Tully harvested timber from it, and claimed title through adverse possession
    \end{itemize}
    
    \textbf{Question} Did Anderson-Tully prove the elements of adverse possession?
    
    \textbf{Holding} Yes, the court held that Anderson-Tully proved adverse possession.
    
    \textbf{Reasoning}
    The court set out the following requirements for proof of adverse possession in Mississippi.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Claim of ownership:} Anderson-Tully treated the disputed land as its own by marking the boundary, harvesting timber, and granting hunting licenses over the parcel.
        \item \textbf{Actual or hostile possession:} Anderson-Tully's timber harvesting and other uses constituted actual possession. The possession was hostile because Anderson-Tully used the land \textit{without} Scott’s permission and in a manner inconsistent with Scott’s ownership.
        \item \textbf{Open, notorious, and visible:} The blue-painted boundary and fence were plainly observable, and people in the community recognized them as marking Anderson-Tully’s property line.
        \item \textbf{Continuous and uninterrupted for the statutory period:} Anderson-Tully marked the boundary in 1969, repainted it in 1986 and 1998, and harvested timber in 1990, 1999, and 2010. Scott’s objection in 2003 came too late. 
        \item \textbf{Exclusive:} The evidence showed that Anderson-Tully used the disputed land after 1969 with intention to possess the land to the exclusion of others, including Smith. 
        \item \textbf{Peaceful:} Anderson-Tully’s possession was not maintained through force or ongoing conflict. 
    \end{itemize}
}

    \note{Recreational Activities}
    {In Kentucky, a court ruled that engaging in recreational activities on the land like hunting, fishing, etc. do not qualify for ``actual possession," in order to reduce fears of property owners that they might lose property rights by allowing recreational users on their land.}

\casebox
{\textit{Carpenter v. Ruperto} (1982)}
{}
{
\textbf{Facts}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Carpenter used a strip of her neighbors’ land as an extension of her yard for more than thirty years.
    \item She knew that the strip was not part of her property.
    \item She argued that hse had acquired this plot of land through adverse possession. 
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Question} Did Carpenter establish adverse possession under a valid claim of right?

\textbf{Holding} No. The Iowa Supreme Court affirmed the denial of her adverse-possession claim due to lack of good faith. 

\textbf{Reasoning} 
\begin{itemize}
    \item Carpenter’s possession was actual, open, exclusive, hostile, and continuous for the statutory period.
    \item But, she failed to prove a good-faith claim of right. 
    \item She knew from the beginning that she did not own the land and had no basis for claiming an interest in it. 
    \item Mere possession for the statutory period cannot create a claim of right for a knowing squatter.
    \item Under Iowa law, adverse possession requires possession under a claim of right or title for at least ten years, which must be made in good faith. 
    \item Knowledge that one’s title is defective is not automatically disqualifying, but knowing that one has no title or legitimate claim is.
\end{itemize}
}

\casebox
{\textit{Howard v. Kunto} (1970)}
{}
{
\textbf{Facts}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Kunto used a waterfront vacation home that was on Howard's land. 
    \item Although Kunto had been in the home for less than a year when the lawsuit happened, combined with his predecessor, they had been occupying the home for decades. 
    \item Because of a surveying error, their deeds technically described the adjacent lot rather than the lot where the house stood. 
    \item Kunto and his predecessors used the property primarily during the summer.
    \item Howard found out through a survey that they had acquired the record title to the land where the Kunto house stood.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Question} 
\begin{itemize}
    \item Does seasonal use prevent possession from being “continuous”?
    \item Can Kunto combine—or tack—his predecessors’ periods of possession to satisfy the statutory period?
\end{itemize} 

\textbf{Holding} 
\begin{itemize}
    \item The court ruled for Kunto, saying that:
    \item No, seasonal use does not defeat continuity
    \item Yes, Kunto could tack his predecessors’ possession
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Reasoning}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Continuous possession:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Continuity does not require someone to occupy land every day. 
        \item The motivation behind proving continuity is whether the possessor used the land as an ordinary owner would, given the property’s nature. 
        \item Because the property was a summer vacation home, summer occupancy, together with the continued existence of the house and improvements, was sufficient.
    \end{itemize}
    
    \item Tacking: 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Successive occupants may combine their periods of possession when there is privity (a voluntary legal relationship such as a transfer by deed or sale). 
        \item Although the deeds contained incorrect legal descriptions, each purchaser voluntarily transferred possession of the home to the next purchaser and genuinely believed they owned it.
        \item Motivation behind privity is recognizing the need for some reasonable connection between successive occupants of the real property.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

}
