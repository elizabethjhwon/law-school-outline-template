\section{Basic Concepts}

\textbf{Common Law Norms}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Specific Intent Crimes:} 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Purpose at the time of commission to carry out some other crime.
        \item Example: Burglary = breaking and entering \textit{with intent} to commit a felony inside. 
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{General Intent Crimes:} 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item No need to prove intent/attendant circumstances.
        \item Example: Trespass = entering a building or other premise purposefully, where such act is unlawful. 
    \end{itemize} 

    \item \textbf{Mixed (Either/Or) Crimes:} 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Jurisdictionally defined.
        \item Example: Bigamy = being married to multiple people.
        \item \textbf{Specific intent:} requires attendant circumstances (being aware of marital status).
        \item \textbf{General intent:} Awareness doesn't matter.
    \end{itemize} 

    \item \textbf{Mistake at common law:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{ignoratia legis neminem excusat}: ignorance or mistake of law is no excuse. 
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{MPC Reforms}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Purpose:} Not a formal legal code, but wanted to clarify legal statutes. 
    \item Frequently cited by courts. 
\end{itemize}

\mpc{2.02 (on mens rea):}
{
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Purposely:} Focuses on purpose of $\Delta$.
    \item \textbf{Knowingly:} $\Delta$ is aware of risks that could come from his conduct.
    \item \textbf{Recklessness:} $\Delta$ knowingly disregards a risk that could result from his conduct.
    \item \textbf{Negligence:} $\Delta$ \textit{should} be aware of substantial risk.
    \item Each element needs to be proved separately regarding $\Delta$'s crime (``elemental approach'').
    \item Hesitant to apply negligence: similar to acts of omission
\end{itemize}
}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Mistake as an exception:
\end{itemize}
\mpc{2.04 (mistake)}{A mistake of fact or law serves as a defense when it prevents the defendant from having the specific mental state required by the crime. (Mistake of fact would be up to the jury to decide.)}

\note{Application of common law norms and MPC}{
\begin{itemize}
    \item Judges will usually impute reasonable common law norms or MPC mens rea standard with the exception of negligence.
    \item Presumption of mens rea is favored. 
\end{itemize}}

\casebox
    {\textit{People v. Acosta} (1991)}
    {\textit{People v. Acosta}, 232 Cal. App. 3d 1375, 284 Cal. Rptr. 117 (Cal. Ct. App. 1991).}
    {
        \textbf{Facts} 
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Acosta was apprehended by police in a stolen vehicle.
            \item Acosta led police on a high-speed chase through Orange County (48 miles).
            \item Acosta drove ``egregiously:" running stop signs and red lights, driving on the wrong side of the street, etc.
            \item Police helicopters helped in tracking Acosta during the chase.
            \item Two police helicopters collided, killing three people (police officers).
            \item Turner (expert) says that accident happened because one pilot violated FAA regulations.
            \item Acosta argues that $\nexists$ sufficient evidence proving he proximately caused the death of his victims, because a crash between airborne vehicles was not a foreseeable result of his conduct.
        \end{itemize}
        
        \textbf{Procedural History}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Trial court jury convicted Acosta of three counts of 2nd degree murder and one count of unlawfully driving or taking a vehicle without consent.
            \item Acosta appealed his conviction of 2nd degree murder in California's Court of Appeals (4th district).
        \end{itemize}
        
        \textbf{Relevant law}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Proximate cause of 2nd degree murder
        \end{itemize}
        
        \textbf{Question}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Was Acosta's actions the ``actual cause" of the victim's injury? 
            \item Equivalently: But for Acosta's actions, would the injury have occurred? 
        \end{itemize}
        
        \textbf{Holding}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Unless an act is an actual cause of the injury, it will not be considered a proximate cause. 
            \item $\exists$ proximate cause here.
        \end{itemize}
        
        \textbf{Discussion (J. Wallin)}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Virtually impossible to have a logical verbal formula that produces uniform results.
            \item Standard should be to exclude extraordinary results and determination of facts to be through ``the common sense of the common man as to common things."
            \item Definition of foreseeability in California: not involving $\Delta$'s state of mind, but rather the objective conditions present when he acts.
            \item But for Acosta fleeing the police, the helicopters wouldn't have been in the position for a crash.
            \item Result of helicopter crash is not highly extraordinary.
            \item Given emotional dynamics of pursuit, $\exists$ ``appreciable probability" that pursuer could act negligently or recklessly in order to catch the $\Delta$.
            \item Mens rea: the court found insufficient proof of malice for a murder conviction, as in Acosta didn't consciously disregard the risk to the helicopter pilots.
        \end{itemize}
        
        \textbf{Dissent (J. Crosby)}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item The law does not assign blame to an otherwise blameworthy actor when neither the intervening negligent conduct nor the risk of harm was foreseeable.
            \item Occupants of helicopter were not within the range of apprehension of a fleeing criminal on the ground.
            \item This \textit{was} a highly extraordinary result.
        \end{itemize}
    }

\casebox
    {\textit{People v. Arzon} (1978)}
    {\textit{People v. Arzon}, 92 Misc. 2d 739, 401 N.Y.S.2d 156 (Sup. Ct. N.Y. County 1978).}
    {
        \textbf{Facts}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item $\Delta$ allegedly set fire to a couch on the fifth floor of an abandoned building.
            \item Firefighters responded but withdrew after failing to control the fire.
            \item During evacuation a separate fire caused smoke that made evacuation dangerous.
            \item Firefighter Celic was injured during the evacuation and later died.
            \item The second fire was also arson, but not sufficient evidence that connected Arzon to it.
            \item Arzon charged with depraved-indifference murder and felony murder.
        \end{itemize}

        \textbf{Procedural History}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item $\Delta$ indicted for two counts of 2nd degree murder and one count of 3rd degree arson.
            \item $\Delta$ moved to dismiss the two murder counts, arguing grand-jury evidence was insufficient and that prosecution had not established a causal relationship with his actions and Celic's death.
        \end{itemize}

        \textbf{Question}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Was there sufficient relationship between the $\Delta$'s actions and Celic's death?
            \item Court: Yes
        \end{itemize}

        \textbf{Holding}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item The $\Delta$'s conduct doesn't need to be the sole and exclusive factor in the victim's death.
            \item The fire set by the $\Delta$ was an indispensable link in the chain of events that caused Celic's death.
        \end{itemize}

        \textbf{Discussion}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textit{People v. Kibbe}: $\Delta$s' conduct was a sufficiently direct cause of victim's ensuing death.
            \item Similar to \textit{Kibbe} and how the $\Delta$s were still charged with murder even though the victim died in a separate incident.
            \item Foreseeable that the firemen would respond to the situation ($\Delta$'s fire) thus exposing them to life-threatening danger.
            \item $\Delta$'s act put Celic in a particularly vulnerable position.
        \end{itemize}
    }

    \casebox
        {\textit{People v. Warner-Lambert Co.} (1980)}
        {\textit{People v. Warner-Lambert Co.}, 414 N.E.2d 660 (N.Y. 1980)}
        {
            \textbf{Facts}
            \begin{itemize}
                \item Warner-Lambert indicted for 2nd degree manslaughter and criminally negligent homicide.
                \item Several employees killed during an explosion at a factory.
                \item $\exists$ evidence that corporation used magnesium stearate, which is potentially explosive, in its manufacturing process.
                \item $\Delta$ knew about explosion hazard.
                \item Speculation on what caused the explosion.
            \end{itemize}

            \textbf{Holding}
            \begin{itemize}
                \item Court held that there was insufficient evidence to establish foreseeability of the immediate cause of the explosion.
                \item Dismissed the indictment.
            \end{itemize}

            \textbf{Discussion}
            \begin{itemize}
                \item Reject prosecution's broad application of proximate cause.
                \item Prosecution's argument is that exposure of risk to death $\rightarrow$ death should be criminally liable.
                \item $\Delta$'s actions must be a \textit{sufficiently direct cause} of the ensuing death.
            \end{itemize}
        }

\casebox
{\textit{Commonwealth v. Mochan} (1955)}
{\textit{Commonwealth v. Mochan}, 177 Pa. Super. 454, 110 A.2d 788 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1955).}
{
\textbf{Facts}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\Delta$, with intention, unlawfully, wickedly, and maliciously made numerous phone calls to the house of Louise Zivkovich during the day and night, to verbally harass her. 
    \item Some members of Zivkovich’s household overheard the calls.
    \item Zivkovich and $\Delta$ were strangers, and she was a married woman.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Procedural History}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The defendant was charged with two indictments and convicted before a judge without a jury.
    \item Defendant appealed, saying that this was not a criminal offense under any Pennsylvania statute, and thus did not constitute a misdemeanor at common law. 
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Law}
\begin{itemize}
    \commonlaw{Principle (Penn.)}{Conduct that “openly outrages decency and is injurious to public morals” may be punished as a common-law misdemeanor.}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Question}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Did Mochan’s repeated obscene and sexually explicit telephone calls constitute a punishable common-law misdemeanor, even though no Pennsylvania statute expressly criminalized that conduct?
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Holding}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Majority: Yes, Pennsylvania’s common law permitted punishment of conduct that seriously injured or tended to injure public morality, even if no statute specifically addressed the conduct.
    \item The Pennsylvania Superior Court affirmed the convictions and sentences. 
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Discussion, Majority (J. Hirt)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pennsylvania recognizes the common law of crimes unless the legislature have abolished it. 
    \item Therefore, an act does not have to be specifically prohibited by statute to be criminal. 
    \item The court acknowledges that no prior Pennsylvania case involved this exact conduct. 
    \item But, the absence of an identical precedent is not decisive, should question whether the conduct could be punished as a misdemeanor under general common-law principles. 
    \item Mochan’s repeated, obscene, and sexually degrading phone calls were more than mere verbal requests for adultery. 
    \item His persistent intentional conduct and potential effect on other people using the telephone line were sufficiently offensive to public morality and resulted in “openly outraged decency” and injured public morals, and thus was punishable as a common-law misdemeanor.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Discussion, Dissent (J. Woodside)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Agreed that Mochan’s conduct was reprehensible but argued that reprehensible conduct $\neq$ not necessarily criminal conduct. 
    \item Emphasized that the legislature has the authority to decide whether conduct is sufficiently harmful to the public to warrant criminal punishment. 
    \item The majority created a new crime through judicial decision when they declared Mochan’s conduct as criminal for the first time. 
    \item Warned that broad standards such as conduct that ``injures or tends to injure the public” or ``outrages decency” give courts too much discretion. 
    \item Courts' decision to impose criminal punishment whenever $\exists$ immoral conduct undermines the legislature’s role in defining crimes.
\end{itemize}
}

\doctrine{\textit{Nulla Poena Sine Lege}, The Legality Principle}
{
No punishment without the law. 
    \question{What values does this principle serve?}
    {
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Fair warning of conduct that could subject one to prosecution
        \item Controlling discretion of police, prosecutors, and juries
    \end{itemize}
    }
This principle bars retroactivity and vagueness, requiring previously established laws and reasonably clear terms. 
}

\commonlaw{How Old Common Law Crimes Apply}
{
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Old common law:} The offense developed over time, was historically recognized, and often received legislative approval or codification.
    \item Most American states continued to apply English common laws, with the exception of when they were specifically repealed by new statutes. 
    \item As more penal laws were enacted, some states abolished all common-law crimes not included in the new statutes.
    \item Beginning in the 1960s, most states replaced these older statutes with modern criminal codes influenced by the Model Penal Code. 
    \item Still, some states continued to preserve traditional common-law definitions.
\end{itemize}
}

\commonlaw{How New Common Law Crimes Apply}
{
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{New common law:} A court declares conduct criminal even though: 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item no statute prohibits it; and
        \item no established precedent previously recognized it as a crime.
    \end{enumerate}
    \item This is what the majority did in Commonwealth v. Mochan.
    \item New common-law crimes raise serious legality and separation-of-powers concerns, and most states have moved away from it (including Pennsylvania.)
    \item Pennsylvania’s current criminal code provides that conduct is not criminal unless made so by statute or another legally authorized provision.
    \item The United States Supreme Court has not held that judicial creation of state crimes is always unconstitutional, but have strongly opposed the creation of new federal common-law crimes. 
    \item In \textit{United States v. Hudson \& Goodwin}, the Court held that federal courts lacked authority to create federal crimes. 
    \item In \textit{United States v. Wiltberger}, Chief Justice Marshall emphasized that the legislature has the power to define crimes and prescribe punishment. 
\end{itemize}
}

\section{Lenity}
\casebox{\textit{McBoyle v. United States} (1931)}
{\textit{McBoyle v. United States}, 283 U.S. 25 (1931).}
{
\textbf{Facts}
\begin{itemize}
    \item McBoyle transported a airplane that he knew was stolen from Illinois to Oklahoma
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Procedural History}
\begin{itemize}
    \item McBoyle was sentenced to serve three years of imprisonment and pay a fine of \$2000. 
    \item McBoyle appealed to SCOTUS, writ of certiorari was granted on the question whether the National Motor Vehicle Theft Act applied to an airplane.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Law}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{National Motor Vehicle Theft Act, Section 2 (a):} The term ``motor vehicle" shall include an automobile, automobile truck, automobile wagon, motor cycle, or any other self-propelled vehicle not designed for running on rails;
    \item \textbf{Section 3:} That whoever shall transport or cause to be transported in interstate or foreign commerce a motor vehicle, knowing the same to have been stolen, shall be punished by a fine of not more than \$5000 or by imprisonment of not more than five years, or both.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Question}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Does the term ``vehicle" in the sense of this act include airplanes?
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Holding}
\begin{itemize}
    \item No, airplanes are not included in this act.
    \item Judgment reversed. 
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Discussion}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Everyday speech: vehicle = thing moving on land.
    \item Theme of the language used in the act refers to vehicles on land. 
    \item Legislative intent: Although airplanes were well-known in 1919, when this statute was passed, they were not mentioned in the reports or in the debates in Congress. 
    \item A fair warning should be given, in terms that the general public would understand, of what the law is. 
    \item Should not extend the statute to include airplanes just because we think a similar policy should apply or on speculation that legislation would have wanted us to interpret the act this way. 
\end{itemize}
}

\casebox{\textit{Wooden v. United States} (2022)}
{\textit{Wooden v. United States}, 595 U.S. 360 (2022).}
{
\textbf{Facts}
\begin{itemize}
    \item In 1997, William Wooden and several accomplices entered a storage facility in Georgia, where they broke through interior walls and stole property from ten storage units. 
    \item Georgia charged Wooden with ten separate burglary counts (one for each storage unit). 
    \item Wooden pleaded guilty to all ten counts and received concurrent sentences. 
    \item In 2014, police found firearms in Wooden’s home. 
    \item Because Wooden had a prior felony conviction, a jury convicted him of being a felon in possession of a firearm under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g). 
    \item Prosecution sought an enhanced sentence under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA). 
    \item The Government argued that Wooden’s ten burglaries counted as ten different occasions.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Procedural History}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The federal district court accepted the Government’s argument that each entry into a different storage unit constituted a separate occasion, applying the ACCA enhancement and sentenced Wooden to 188 months—nearly sixteen years. 
    \item The Sixth Circuit affirmed, reasoned that the burglaries occurred at distinct moments in time and that Wooden could not have been in multiple storage units simultaneously. 
    \item The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve how to interpret ACCA’s “occasions” clause. 
    \item The Supreme Court reversed the Sixth Circuit and held that Wooden’s ten burglaries occurred on one occasion, so they counted as only one ACCA predicate conviction.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Armed Criminal Career Act (ACCA):} provides a fifteen-year mandatory minimum sentence for a person convicted under Section 922(g) who has three prior convictions for specified violent felonies or serious drug offenses “committed on occasions different from one another.” 

\textbf{Question}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Did Wooden's ten burglary convictions, arising from his entry into ten storage units in the same facility during one uninterrupted criminal episode, constitute ten different occasions for purposes of ACCA?
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Holding}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wooden’s ten burglaries occurred on a single occasion. 
    \item Because they did not occur on occasions different from one another, they counted as only one prior conviction under ACCA. 
    \item Wooden therefore did not qualify for the ACCA enhancement.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Discussion, Majority (J. Kagan)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ordinary meaning} of ``occasion”: one would say that Wooden burglarized ten storage units ``on one occasion,” not that he burglarized one unit on one occasion, another unit on a second occasion, and so on. 
    \item An occasion can include multiple activities that occur at different times, like how a wedding includes a ceremony, dinner, and dancing even though those activities are not simultaneous. 
    \item ``Occasion" = better understood as an episode or event.
    \item Factors to be considered when considering the definition of ``occasion": 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item the timing of the offenses; 
        \item whether the conduct was continuous or interrupted; 
        \item the location of the offenses; 
        \item the similarity or relationship between the offenses; and 
        \item whether the offenses were part of a common scheme or purpose.
    \end{itemize}

    \item For all these factors, Wooden's crimes constituted one occasion, rather than multiple.
    \item \textbf{Legislative history and purpose:} Congress added the occasions requirement after an earlier case treated multiple convictions arising from one criminal episode as separate within the meaning of the ACCA. 
    \item The amendment was designed to prevent someone from becoming an armed career criminal based on multiple convictions arising from a single criminal episode (like Wooden!).
\end{itemize}

\doctrine{Rule of Lenity}{Reasonable doubts about the meaning or application of a criminal law should be resolved in favor of the defendant. Historically, the rule developed in English courts based partly on the assumption that Parliament would speak clearly when it intended to impose severe punishment. In the United States, lenity took on additional constitutional importance because it protects due process and fair notice, and the separation of powers.}

\textbf{Discussion, Concurrence (J. Gorsuch)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Criticized majority’s multi-factor test as insufficiently clear and predictable.
    \item The test gives courts many factors but little guidance about things like which factors matter most, how to weigh the factors, what qualifies as a substantial gap in time, what qualifies as a significant intervening event, and how courts should resolve close cases.
    \item Worried that this approach would allow different judges to reach different conclusions based on differing intuitions. 
    \item The fact that lower court judges in Wooden’s case concluded that the burglaries occurred on different occasions = reasonable disagreement was possible.
    \item \textbf{Rule of Lenity:} Because reasonable judges could disagree about whether Wooden’s burglaries occurred on one occasion or many, lenity required judgment in Wooden’s favor.
    \item Especially important because ACCA’s occasions clause gives courts little guidance and doesn't define the word ``occasion.” 
    \item Since ambiguity remains, courts need a rule of decision, and lenity supplies that rule.
    \item So, the court shouldn't decide based on which factors matter most, but instead should look at rule of lenity because the ACCA clause was reasonably ambiguous.
\end{itemize}
}

\section{Proof Beyond a Reasonable Doubt}

\casebox{\textit{In re Winship} (1970)}
{\textit{In re Winship}, 397 U.S. 358 (1970)}
{
\textbf{Facts}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Winship (12 yo juvenile) accused of entering a locker and stealing \$112 from a woman’s pocketbook. 
    \item Under New York law, a juvenile was delinquent if he commits an act that would constitute a crime if committed by an adult. 
    \item The juvenile court found that Winship had committed an act that would constitute larceny. 
    \item The court applied preponderance of evidence standard. 
    \item The judge acknowledged that the evidence might not have established guilt beyond a reasonable doubt but found it sufficient under the lower standard. 
    \item Winship was ordered to be in a training school for at least eighteen months, with possible extensions until his eighteenth birthday (potentially six years of confinement).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Procedural History}
\begin{itemize}
    \item New York Family Court found Winship delinquent. 
    \item The Appellate Division affirmed. 
    \item The New York Court of Appeals affirmed and upheld the constitutionality of using the preponderance standard in juvenile delinquency proceedings. 
    \item Supreme Court granted review and reversed.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Law} \textbf{The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment} requires the government to prove every fact necessary to establish a criminal offense beyond a reasonable doubt.

\textbf{Question}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Does due process require the prosecution to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that a juvenile committed an act that would constitute a crime if committed by an adult?
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Holding}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Yes, the Due Process Clause requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt when the juvenile is accused of conduct that would constitute a crime if committed by an adult.
    \item Held that requiring proof beyond a reasonable doubt would not destroy the beneficial features of the juvenile system.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Discussion, Majority (J. Brennan)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Emphasized that the reasonable-doubt standard is a fundamental safeguard against factual error because it reduces the risk that an innocent person will be convicted and reinforces the presumption of innocence.
    \item Reasons for this high standard:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item A criminal adjudication may deprive a person of liberty. 
        \item A conviction carries serious stigma. 
        \item The reasonable-doubt standard helps preserve public confidence in the criminal justice system. 
        \item The standard reflects the judgment that convicting an innocent person is worse than allowing a guilty person to go free.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Rejected New York’s argument that juvenile proceedings were different because they were labeled civil and intended to rehabilitate rather than punish. 
    \item Found that these labels did not eliminate due process protections when the proceeding could result in years of confinement and when the case death with criminal law.
    \item Requiring proof beyond a reasonable doubt would not destroy the beneficial features of the juvenile system.
    \item The higher standard applied only to the adjudicatory determination of whether the juvenile committed the alleged criminal act, and the states could still maintain informal procedures, confidentiality, etc.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Discussion, Concurrence (J. Harlan)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Focused on the different consequences of factual error. 
    \item In civil cases, the law generally treats an erroneous judgment for one party as roughly comparable to an erroneous judgment for the other, but criminal cases are different because society considers it substantially worse to convict an innocent person than to acquit a guilty person.
    \item The beyond-reasonable-doubt standard therefore reflects a fundamental value judgment that the legal system should tolerate some guilty people going free in order to reduce the risk of convicting the innocent. 
    \item Juvenile courts could retain their distinctive procedures and rehabilitative goals, as long as the government proved the alleged criminal conduct beyond a reasonable doubt.
\end{itemize}
}

\note{Basis of the reasonable-doubt requirement}
{
\begin{itemize}
    \item J. Harlan: Social disutility of convicting an innocent person is always worse than releasing a guilty person.
    \item William Blackstone: Better that 10 guilty people escape rather than one innocent person suffer.
    \item If we don't rely on the utility comparison, would that mean the reasonable-doubt requirement is not justified, or is there an underlying belief that people have a fundamental right not to suffer punishment without reliable proof of fault?
\end{itemize}
}

\note{How burden-of-proof problems arise}
{
The issue can arise:
\begin{itemize}
    \item at the close of the prosecution’s case;
    \item at the close of all evidence;
    \item during the jury’s deliberations; and
    \item on appeal for insufficient evidence.
\end{itemize}
}

\note{Reasonable doubt as a matter of law}
{
\begin{itemize}
    \item A judge should remove a case from the jury only when no reasonable juror could find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. 
    \item If reasonable jurors could fairly reach either conclusion, the issue belongs to the jury. 
    \item The judge should not decide credibility or weigh evidence as a substitute for the jury.
\end{itemize}
}

\note{Explaining reasonable doubt (to a jury)}
{
\begin{itemize}
    \item Jury instructions must not dilute the reasonable-doubt standard. 
    \item Courts have criticized numerical explanations, such as saying reasonable doubt means “seven and a half out of ten,” because reasonable doubt is qualitative rather than mathematical. 
    \item Courts have also rejected language such as ``grave uncertainty” or ``moral certainty” when it suggests a lower standard than the Constitution requires.
    \item Some courts prefer to give the jury no explanation at all. 
    \item A traditionally accepted defintion of reasonable doubt is the following Cal. Penal Code definition, but even here, what does ``moral evidence" or ``moral certainty" mean? 
\end{itemize}

\statutebox{Cal. Penal Code Section 1096 (1994)}
{
\textbf{Reasonable doubt:} It is not a mere possible doubt; because everything relating to human affairs is open to some possible or imaginary doubt. It is that state of the case, which, after the entire comparison and consideration of all the evidence, leaves the minds of jurors in that condition that they cannot say they feel an abiding conviction of the truth of the charge.
}
